## Don't multitask
There are absolutely times when multitasking is necessary, but try to minimize them as much as possible. *Especially* in your personal time. 
If your evening commute consists of recording voice notes while dodging E-bikes, while skipping through tracks for the music you have blasting in the background, you're probably not able to appreciate the setting sun. 
If your Saturday morning routine consists of watching AMTA/Minecraft parkour on your phone while eating, with some anime filler arc playing in the background, you're probably barely registering the notes of hazelnut the premium coffee you were so excited to try out (These are all things I have done, so no judgement). I don't mean to imply that everyone needs to be registering the notes of hazelnut in their coffee. I only mean that our senses our limited. When we focus on more than one thing, parts of each of those things are lost. When I'm lucky enough to be faced with something I really enjoy or value, I find it fulfilling to five it my full attention--to make the most out of that one experience.

Multitasking is part of a larger subject: *focus*. If we recognize a task as something more abstract than a physical activity, the concept of multitasking can apply to all sorts of mental load. I think the detriments of multitasking in this context is something very intuitive to us--if there's an important email you're waiting to hear back on, or if there's an important conversation you're anticipating in the near future, it can be hard to give any one thing your full attention. These sorts of thoughts (the kind that redirect us from our current area of focus) are often called distractions; distraction sort of implies there's something that you *ought* to be focusing on, so I'm not sure how I like the word in the context of leisure. If something pulls you away for a second, don't get mad at your self, give that thought some time to permeate, and you'll eventually find yourself back to whatever you were working on. But remember, don't multitask. If you're doing something mentally demanding like playing a video game, watching tv or reading a book, then pause for a second, if you can, and come back to it. This is your leisure time, you didn't punch in to watch TV.

## Be Bored
When I was a kid (not that long ago) I was bored all the time. I remember being the last one to get picked up from soccer practice, sitting in the waiting room for the dentist, or going through a long tunnel on a road trip where the radio cuts out and its eerily quiet for 3 or so minutes. They were awkward little moments. It's only after losing these moments, and taking them back that I realized how important they were. When there isn't any external stimuli, your mind turns inward. You get a little moment of reflection. To just exist in the world.

Around a year ago I was visiting my grandmother for a holiday. The family had all left and my girlfriend and I were the last ones there. We left for 10 minutes to grab something from the car and came back in. When we opened the door, we realized that my grandmother had been sitting alone, with no music, no tv, no anything for the 10 minutes we were gone... She was just looking around, waiting. We dropped off what we needed, and hugged her goodbye. On the way to the car my girlfriend commented, "She looked so sad when we came in." That moment sort of stuck with me. Looking back, I don't think she was sad at all, maybe a little sentimental, but I'm sure she was just soaking in the joy of the evening. It's funny how foreign that behavior seemed to us at the time. At what point did we lose these little moments of boredom? And how do we take them back? I'm sure you can already guess.

### Smartphones
*Dun Dun Dunnnnnn.* Here's the part where I try not to sound performative. A couple months ago I would tell you about how smartphones are evil, social media is trying to take over your mind, and that we should all go back to using flip phones. I've seen a lot of similar claims online, and I think there's some warrant to them, but the conversation is so much more nuanced. The reason people argue so strongly against smartphone use is easy to visualize from my previous examples. Imagine your mom is running late to pick you up from soccer practice. You get slightest hint of boredom and... You pop open Instagram. *Bam!* Boredom gone. The same could be said for just about any in-between moment: train platform, elevator ride... Hell, even a moment of slight discomfort: Conversation die down with a friend over lunch? *Bam!* reddit, *Bam!* let me clear my spam folder. Boredom gone. I was even a little productive.
